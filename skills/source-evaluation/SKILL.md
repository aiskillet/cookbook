# Source Evaluation

Not all sources are equal, and confident writing isn't evidence. Before you rely on a source, interrogate it — the cost of a wrong "fact" compounds through everything built on it.

## When to Activate
- Researching or fact-checking a claim
- Deciding whether to cite/trust a source
- Evaluating AI output, blogs, vendor content, or news

## The questions (CRAAP-style)
- **Who** wrote it? Author/organization credentials, expertise, and *incentive*. A vendor's blog on why you need vendors is suspect.
- **Evidence?** Does it cite primary sources and data, or just assert? Can you trace claims to origin?
- **When?** Recency matters — especially in fast-moving fields. Check the date and whether it's been superseded.
- **Bias/purpose?** Inform, sell, or persuade? Follow the money and the agenda.
- **Corroboration?** Do independent, credible sources agree? One source is a lead, not a fact.

## Source hierarchy (rough)
1. **Primary** — original data, papers, official docs, the actual spec/code.
2. **Reputable secondary** — peer-reviewed, established outlets, recognized experts.
3. **Tertiary/aggregators** — good for orientation and finding primaries, not final authority.
4. **Anonymous/uncited/AI-generated** — treat as unverified until corroborated.

## Red flags
- No author, no date, no citations.
- Claims that are too clean / too aligned with the author's interest.
- Circular sourcing (everyone citing one unverified origin).
- Emotional language substituting for evidence.

## Practically
- **Trace to the primary source** — don't cite the summary of a summary.
- **Triangulate** important claims across independent sources.
- **Note confidence** — mark what's well-established vs. thinly sourced.

## Checklist
- [ ] Author authority + incentive checked
- [ ] Claims backed by traceable evidence
- [ ] Recency verified; not superseded
- [ ] Bias/purpose identified
- [ ] Key claims corroborated independently; traced to primary
